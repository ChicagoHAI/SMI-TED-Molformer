\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\para{\molm and \smi.}
We study two SMILES-based chemistry foundation models: \molm~\citep{ross2022largescalechemicallanguagerepresentations} and \smi~\citep{soares2024smi}. Both are transformer models pretrained without explicit geometric supervision and both use rotary linear attention, making them a useful pair for comparing how different pretraining choices shape internal chemical representations.
\molm is an encoder-only model trained with a masked language modeling objective. It produces token-level hidden states and a pooled molecular representation for downstream prediction. In the original paper, it is evaluated on a range of complex molecular prediction tasks which requires understanding more than just basic properties, and it is argued that attention captures aspects of molecular structure \citep{ross2022largescalechemicallanguagerepresentations,wu2018moleculenet}. \smi, by contrast, uses an encoder--decoder design with both masked token prediction and sequence reconstruction. Its latent representation is therefore trained not only to support token-level inference, but also to retain enough information to reconstruct the input SMILES. The original \smi is evaluated on similar tasks as \molm \citep{soares2024smi,wu2018moleculenet}. \citet{sharma2025foundation} build on \smi for downstream scientific prediction and also includes an interpretability analysis by relating chemical descriptors to model behavior through correlation. 

\para{Chemical Properties and SMILES String.}
To probe learned structural representations, each atom is annotated with eight RDKit-derived properties. These should be understood as local chemical descriptors, not molecule-level prediction targets: they encode chemically meaningful structural information, but are not sufficient to determine higher-level molecular behavior on their own.
We also distinguish these atom-level descriptors from \textbf{geometric properties}, which are not directly encoded in SMILES and cannot be read from token identity alone. 
Within the probed descriptors, we distinguish between \textbf{surface-form properties}, whose labels are directly readable from individual SMILES tokens, and \textbf{contextual properties}, which require the encoder to integrate information across the molecular graph. Atom type and aromaticity are surface-form since element symbols are encoded as distinct tokens, and aromatic atoms are marked by lowercase letters in SMILES notation. By contrast, hybridization, degree, total valence, ring membership, formal charge, and chiral tag cannot be read from individual tokens in isolation. They are contextual and therefore provide more direct tests of what the encoder has learned beyond its tokenization. Detailed definitions of all properties are provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:properties}.
