\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
Recent transformer-based chemical foundation models trained on SMILES strings have demonstrated strong performance on diverse downstream molecular tasks. However, it remains unclear what chemical information these models internally represent, where such information emerges across layers, and how it is utilized during computation. This motivates a mechanistic investigation of the internal representations learned by chemical foundation models.

This question is particularly important because SMILES strings do not explicitly encode many chemically meaningful properties.
While local syntactic information such as atom identity is directly visible from the token sequence, higher-level chemical structure---including stereochemistry, valence, ring structure, and 3D geometry---must instead be inferred from context.

In this work, we study the internal representations of two widely used chemistry foundation models, \molm and \smi. We first analyze whether attention patterns directly encode molecular geometry by measuring per-layer per-head correlations between attention weights and inverse 3D interatomic distance.
We then probe hidden states for atom-level chemical properties ranging from surface-form descriptors to contextual structural features requiring graph-level reasoning.
Finally, we perform causal interventions on probe directions to test whether these representations are merely linearly recoverable or are functionally used by the model during computation.

While \molm suggests that transformer attention strongly aligns with interatomic proximity, our results show that this correlation is weak and highly non-uniform, with modest correlation observed only at short interatomic distances. However, several contextual chemical properties are strongly linearly recoverable from hidden states, particularly in \smi. This is consistent with the strong downstream performance reported by both \molm and \smi. Moreover, causal interventions reveal that these representations become functionally usable substantially earlier in \smi than in \molm.
Together, these findings suggest that successful SMILES pretraining does not necessarily produce spatially grounded or uniformly organized chemical representations, highlighting a gap between downstream predictive performance and the internal structure of learned representations.
