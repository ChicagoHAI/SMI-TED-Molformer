\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
Scientific foundation models have recently emerged as a powerful paradigm across domains including chemistry, biology, and materials science. In chemistry, recent transformer-based foundation models trained on SMILES strings have demonstrated strong performance across diverse downstream molecular tasks and claim to encode meaningful chemical structure and molecular properties within their learned representations. However, it remains unclear what chemical information these models internally represent, where such information emerges across layers, and how it is utilized during computation. This motivates a mechanistic investigation of the internal representations learned by chemical foundation models, as well as a comparative analysis of how different models organize and use chemical information.

This question is particularly important because SMILES strings do not explicitly encode many chemically meaningful properties. While local syntactic information such as atom identity is directly visible from the token sequence, higher-level chemical structure—including stereochemistry, valence, ring structure, and 3D geometry—must instead be inferred from context. As a result, it remains unclear whether successful downstream performance reflects structured chemical representations or weaker statistical shortcuts learned during pretraining.

In this paper, we study the internal representations of two widely used chemistry foundation models, \molm~\citep{ross2022largescalechemicallanguagerepresentations} and \smi~\citep{soares2024smi}. We first analyze whether attention patterns encode molecular geometry by measuring correlations between attention weights and inverse 3D interatomic distance. We then probe hidden states for atom-level chemical properties ranging from surface-form descriptors to contextual structural features. Furthermore, we use causal interventions on probe directions to test whether these representations are only linearly recoverable or functionally used during computation.

Our main findings are as follows. First, contrary to claims in \molm, we find that attention-distance correlations are weak and highly non-uniform, with modest correlation observed only at short interatomic distances. Second, consistent with \molm and \smi, we observe strong downstream predictive performance for both models. Third, we find that several contextual chemical properties are substantially more linearly accessible in \smi than in \molm despite similar downstream performance. Furthermore, we find that these representations become functionally usable substantially earlier in \smi than in \molm.

Together, these findings suggest that strong downstream chemical performance does not require attention mechanisms that explicitly track molecular geometry. Instead, chemically meaningful information emerges primarily in hidden-state representations and is organized differently across models.
