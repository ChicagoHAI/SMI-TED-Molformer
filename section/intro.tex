\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\input{figure/figure1.tex}

Foundation models pretrained on large scientific datasets are rapidly transforming prediction across the natural sciences. They have shown strong performance in proteins~\citep{lin2023esm2,jumper2021highly}, genomics~\citep{nguyen2024evo}, and weather forecasting~\citep{lam2023graphcast}. Specifically, chemistry foundation models pretrained on large-scale SMILES corpora, including ChemBERTa~\citep{chithrananda2020chemberta}, \molm~\citep{ross2022largescalechemicallanguagerepresentations}, \smi~\citep{soares2024smi}, and ChemFM~\citep{cai2026chemfm}, now serve as standard backbones for molecular property prediction, ADMET screening, and materials discovery~\citep{wu2018moleculenet,harod2025effichem,sharma2025foundation}. Despite operating directly on molecular text without explicit geometric supervision, these models achieve strong performance across a wide range of downstream chemistry tasks.

However, strong downstream performance does not imply that we understand what these models have learned internally. SMILES strings do not explicitly encode many chemically meaningful properties. For example, atom identity is directly visible from the token sequence, but higher-level structure, such as stereochemistry, valence, ring structure, and 3D geometry, must instead be inferred from context. It is therefore unclear whether strong downstream performance reflects structured chemical representations or weaker statistical patterns learned during pretraining. Because chemistry foundation models are typically deployed as black-box feature extractors, three concrete questions about their representations remain open: \emph{(i)} do attention heads track 3D molecular structure? \emph{(ii)} at what depth do contextual chemical properties such as ring membership or chirality emerge in the residual stream? and \emph{(iii)} are these representations actively used during computation, or merely co-encoded with the eventual prediction?

To answer these questions, we apply the same set of analyses to two of the most widely used SMILES encoders, \molm and \smi. The models are architecturally similar as both of them use 12-layer transformer encoders with 768-dimensional hidden states and rotary linear attention. This makes them a useful comparison pair for isolating how different pretraining designs shape internal chemical representations. Across both models, we perform three complementary experiments: (1) attention–distance correlation across all heads to test whether attention reflects 3D molecular geometry; (2) linear probing of eight per-atom RDKit-derived chemical properties~\cite{rdkit} to distinguish surface-level token information from contextual chemical structure; and (3) causal interventions along probe directions to test whether decoded properties are actively used during computation beyond being linearly recoverable.

We find that the two models, despite their architectural similarity, organize chemical information in substantially different ways. Attention in both encoders only weakly tracks 3D molecular geometry, with per-head Spearman correlations below $0.3$ and highly non-uniform across heads, contradicting prior claims that \molm attention encodes spatial structure. Surface-level properties readable from SMILES tokens (atom type, aromaticity) reach $100\%$ probe accuracy at the embedding layer in both models, while contextual properties that require aggregating across the molecular graph (ring membership, degree, chiral tag, hybridization, total valence) only become linearly recoverable inside the encoder, and \smi recovers them substantially more cleanly than \molm. Causal interventions along probe directions reveal a further gap. Even where \molm achieves high probe accuracy, those directions only become functionally usable near the top of the encoder, whereas in \smi the same properties drive intervention success to $\approx 1.0$ by mid-depth. Linear recoverability and causal use are therefore not the same thing, and the depth at which contextual chemistry becomes computationally usable differs substantially across pretraining designs.

\noindent To summarize, our main contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We pair layerwise linear probing with causal interventions along probe directions to study chemical foundation models, going beyond the correlational attention and embedding analyses used in prior work on SMILES encoders.
    \item We apply this framework to \molm and \smi across eight per-atom RDKit-derived chemical properties on QM9, separating surface-level chemistry exposed by SMILES tokens from contextual chemistry that requires aggregation across the molecular graph.
    \item We show that linear recoverability and causal use are distinct, and that two architecturally similar SMILES encoders differ substantially in how cleanly contextual chemistry is encoded and at what depth it becomes computationally usable, indicating that pretraining design shapes not only what information is present but when it is usable during computation.
\end{itemize}
