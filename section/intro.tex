\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\input{figure/figure1.tex}

Foundation models pretrained on large scientific datasets are rapidly transforming prediction across the natural sciences. For example, they have achieved promising results in domains including proteins~\citep{lin2023esm2,jumper2021highly}, genomics~\citep{nguyen2024evo}, and weather forecasting~\citep{lam2023graphcast}. Specifically, chemistry foundation models pretrained on large-scale SMILES corpora, including ChemBERTa~\citep{chithrananda2020chemberta}, \molm~\citep{ross2022largescalechemicallanguagerepresentations}, \smi~\citep{soares2024smi}, and ChemFM~\citep{cai2026chemfm}, now serve as standard backbones for molecular property prediction, ADMET screening, and materials discovery~\citep{wu2018moleculenet,harod2025effichem,sharma2025foundation}. Despite operating directly on molecular text without explicit geometric supervision, these models achieve strong performance across a wide range of downstream chemistry tasks.

However, strong downstream performance does not imply that we understand what these models have learned internally. SMILES strings do not explicitly encode many chemically meaningful properties. For example, atom identity and aromaticity are directly visible from the token sequence, but higher-level structure, such as stereochemistry, valence, ring structure, and 3D geometry, must instead be inferred from context. Thus, it is unclear whether strong downstream performance reflects meaningful chemical representations or weaker statistical patterns learned during pretraining. Because molecule models are typically deployed as black-box feature extractors, three concrete questions about their internal mechanism remain open: \emph{(i)} do attention heads track 3D molecular structure? \emph{(ii)} at what depth do contextual chemical properties such as ring membership or chirality emerge in the residual stream? and \emph{(iii)} are these representations actively used during computation, or merely co-encoded with the eventual prediction?

To answer these questions, we apply the same set of analyses to two of the most widely used SMILES encoders, \molm and \smi. The models are architecturally similar as both of them use 12-layer transformer encoders with 768-dimensional hidden states and rotary linear attention. This makes them a useful comparison pair for isolating how different pretraining designs shape internal chemical representations. Across both models, we perform three complementary experiments: (1) attention–distance correlation across all heads to test whether attention reflects 3D molecular geometry; (2) linear probing of eight per-atom RDKit-derived chemical properties~\cite{rdkit} to distinguish surface-level token information from contextual chemical structure; and (3) causal interventions along probe directions to test whether decoded properties are actively used during computation beyond being linearly recoverable (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}).

We find that the two models, despite their architectural similarity, organize chemical information in different ways. Attention in both encoders only weakly tracks 3D molecular geometry, with highly non-uniform Spearman correlations below $0.3$ across all layer-head pairs, which contrast with prior claims that \molm attention encodes spatial structure~\cite{ross2022largescalechemicallanguagerepresentations}. Surface-form properties directly encoded in SMILES tokens, such as atom type and aromaticity, reach nearly $100\%$ probe accuracy at the embedding layer in both models. In contrast, contextual properties that require aggregating information across the molecular graph emerge later in the encoder, with \smi encoding them substantially more cleanly than \molm. Causal interventions reveal a further distinction between representations that are merely decodable and those that are functionally used during computation. 
% Even when \molm achieves high probe accuracy, the corresponding directions only become functionally usable near the top of the encoder. In \smi, the same properties drive intervention success to approximately $1.0$ by mid-depth. Thus, linear recoverability and causal use are not equivalent, and the depth at which contextual chemical information becomes computationally usable differs across pretraining designs.\footnote{Code Available: \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/smimolm-76E0/}}

\noindent To summarize, our main contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We perform a mechanistic interpretability study of \molm and \smi, analyzing how they represent chemical structure and properties from SMILES strings. Revisiting prior claims that molecular attention captures spatial structure, we show that attention patterns correlate only weakly with true 3D interatomic distance despite strong downstream chemistry performance.
    
    \item Using layerwise probing and causal interventions across RDKit-derived atomic properties, we show that surface-form properties are directly recoverable from token embeddings, while contextual chemical properties are encoded much more cleanly and become causally usable earlier in \smi than in \molm.
    
    \item Our results suggest that linear decodability and causal usage capture different aspects of representation learning in molecular foundation models, motivating more careful interpretation of probing results in scientific domains.
\end{itemize}
