Molecular property prediction is a crucial step in drug discovery and materials
science. Traditional approaches rely on hand-crafted molecular fingerprints or graph
neural networks that explicitly encode molecular structure.
More recently, transformer-based language models pretrained on large corpora of
SMILES strings have emerged as a powerful alternative, learning rich molecular
representations without requiring explicit structural inductive biases.

Despite impressive benchmark performance, the representational mechanisms of these
models are not well understood. Do attention heads specialise for particular
chemical substructures? Which layers encode atom-level chemical properties? To what
extent does the learned representation reflect three-dimensional molecular geometry?
These questions are important both for scientific understanding and for the
trustworthy deployment of molecular AI in high-stakes domains. \xiaoyan{I think there are works about understanding the structures.}

Prior interpretability analyses of chemical foundation models have mostly focused on attention-based correlations, the structure of final-layer embeddings, or correlations between chemical descriptors and model outputs. These analyses provide useful evidence that chemical information is present, but they do not show how such information is distributed across layers or whether it is functionally used during computation. In this paper, we take a step toward a more mechanistic account by studying two chemical language models, \molm and \smi, at the level of their internal representations.

Concretely, we make three contributions. First, we perform a systematic attention--distance analysis across all layers and heads in both models, providing a more thorough test of whether attention aligns with 3D molecular geometry. Second, we use layerwise linear probes to track how atom-level chemical properties emerge across depth and to distinguish token-level features from contextual chemical information. Third, we apply causal intervention on probe directions to test whether decoded chemical features are merely accessible or are actively used by later computation. Together, these analyses allow us to compare not only what chemical information is represented, but how it is organized and when it becomes usable.
