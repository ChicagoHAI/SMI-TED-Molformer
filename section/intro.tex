\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Foundation models pretrained on large scientific datasets are rapidly transforming prediction across the natural sciences. Protein language models such as ESM-2~\citep{lin2023esm2} and AlphaFold~\citep{jumper2021highly} achieve strong performance on protein structure and function prediction, genomic foundation models such as Evo~\citep{nguyen2024evo} model regulatory DNA sequences at scale, and weather models such as GraphCast~\citep{lam2023graphcast} rival traditional numerical forecasting systems. Across domains, large pretrained transformers replace originally hand-engineered features with representations learned directly from raw scientific data.

Specifically, chemistry foundation models pretrained on large-scale SMILES corpora, including ChemBERTa~\citep{chithrananda2020chemberta}, \molm~\citep{ross2022largescalechemicallanguagerepresentations}, \smi~\citep{soares2024smi}, and ChemFM~\citep{cai2026chemfm}, now serve as standard backbones for molecular property prediction, ADMET screening, and materials discovery~\citep{wu2018moleculenet,harod2025effichem,sharma2025foundation}. Despite operating directly on molecular text without explicit geometric supervision, these models achieve strong performance across a wide range of downstream chemistry tasks.

However, strong downstream performance does not imply that we understand what these models have learned internally. Because chemistry foundation models are typically deployed as black-box feature extractors, fundamental questions about their representations remain open: Do attention heads track 3D structure? At what depth do contextual properties such as ring membership or chirality emerge? And are these representations actively used during computation? These questions are especially important because SMILES strings do not explicitly encode many chemically meaningful properties. While atom identity is directly visible from the token sequence, higher-level structure—including stereochemistry, valence, ring structure, and 3D geometry—must instead be inferred from context. Consequently, it remains unclear whether strong downstream performance reflects genuinely structured chemical representations or weaker statistical patterns learned during pretraining.

This motivates a mechanistic investigation of how chemical foundation models internally represent, organize, and use chemical information, as well as how these representations differ across models.

We apply the same analyses to two of the most widely used SMILES encoders, \molm and \smi. The models are architecturally similar — both use 12-layer transformer encoders with 768-dimensional hidden states and rotary linear attention — making them a useful comparison pair for isolating how different pretraining designs shape internal chemical representations. Across both models, we perform three complementary experiments: (1) layerwise attention–distance correlation across all heads to test whether attention reflects 3D molecular geometry; (2) layerwise linear probing of eight per-atom RDKit-derived chemical properties to distinguish surface-level token information from contextual chemical structure; and (3) causal interventions along probe directions to test whether decoded properties are actively used during computation beyond being linearly recoverable.

Our main findings are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Attention exhibits weak alignment with 3D molecular geometry in both models.} Contrary to claims in \molm, we find that attention-distance correlations are weak ($< 0.3$) and highly non-uniform across all layers and heads of both models, with \smi marginally stronger than \molm. 
    \item \textbf{Surface-level chemistry is encoded in embedding; contextual chemistry is not.} Properties directly exposed by SMILES syntax (atom type, aromaticity) reach $100\%$ probe accuracy at layer~0 in both models. Contextual chemical properties requiring structural information beyond individual tokens (ring membership, degree, chiral tag, hybridization, and valence) only become linearly recoverable within the encoder.
    \item \textbf{\smi{} encodes contextual properties more accurately than \molm{}.} \smi{} reaches near-perfect probe accuracy by mid-depth, whereas \molm{} achieves substantially lower accuracy across all atom-level properties. \anita{can you add smth here?}
    \item \textbf{The same chemical properties become usable at different depths across the two models.} Causal intervention success along probe directions reaches $\approx 1.0$ by mid-layers in \smi{}, but only near the top of the encoder in \molm{}. This suggests that linearly recoverable information is not necessarily functionally used immediately, and that the depth at which representations become computationally usable differs substantially across pretraining designs.
\end{itemize}
