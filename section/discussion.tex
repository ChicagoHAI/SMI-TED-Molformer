\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:res-disc}

\subsection{Chemical information can be present without being organized in a human-like way.}

Strong performance on chemistry tasks is often interpreted as evidence that a model has learned meaningful chemical structure, since many downstream predictions require reasoning about molecular properties and interactions. However, our results show that high predictive performance does not necessarily imply that chemical information is represented in a structured or human-like way. Although prior work suggested that attention in molecular language models may capture spatial relationships between atoms, we find that attention patterns exhibit only weak alignment with true 3D geometry. Instead, most chemically relevant information appears in the hidden representations rather than the attention mechanism itself. Moreover, the organization of this information differs substantially across models. We also show that some properties become detectable by probes before they are causally used by later layers, indicating that representation accessibility and computational usage emerge at different stages of the network.

These differences matter because downstream scientific applications often require more than accurate prediction alone. In practice, we may want molecular models to organize chemical knowledge in ways that support compositional reasoning, reliable transfer, and faithful interpretation. Tasks such as ADMET prediction, molecular binding, and reactivity frequently require combining multiple local and non-local chemical constraints across several computational steps. Representations that become accessible and causally usable earlier in the network can serve as intermediate features for later computation. This may be especially important in transfer settings where molecular encoders are used through frozen embeddings, shallow predictors, parameter-efficient adaptation, or intermediate-layer readouts rather than fully end-to-end retraining so that the downstream model can still capture the information.~\cite{honda2019smiles,rollins2024molprop,cai2026chemfm,harod2025effichem,pinto2025unlocking}. Under these constraints, downstream performance may depend not only on whether chemical information is present somewhere in the model, but also on how cleanly and causally that information is organized.


\subsection{Limitations of probing in scientific foundation models}

Related to the previous section, our results suggest that scientific foundation models may learn and organize domain knowledge in ways that differ from human intuition, which also changes how interpretability tools should be applied to these models. 

Standard probing methods can become difficult to interpret in this setting due to biased scientific datasets, domain-specific tokenization, different training objectives, and the distinct scientific questions we care about. Chemistry atom-level datasets are naturally biased toward certain classes because most atoms in nature are carbon, most carbons are non-aromatic, and most chiral centers carry a single configuration. This might skew the representation space so that a probe can find a direction the encoder does not actually use. Our results expose this gap in three concrete forms. First, on atom type, both \molm and \smi achieve near-perfect probe accuracy at every layer, yet intervention along the probe direction succeeds in \molm only at the very top of the encoder and never in \smi for atom type (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-atom-type}). This aligns what \citet{park2024linear} suggested that linear directions in a model's \emph{output} space need not coincide with the \emph{input} directions later layers use. Second, \molm's chiral-tag probe stays at the $0.795$ majority baseline at every depth, yet steering still flips the readout at the top of the encoder. With no real linear feature to perturb, this nominal success is the false-positive failure mode that \citet{hewitt2019designing} introduced control tasks to detect, and an instance of the encoded-versus-used bug catalogued in NLP probing~\citep{antverg2022pitfalls,elazar2021amnesic}. Third, the L11 dip we report in \smi shows that even a clean probe direction can live in a basis the next encoder block does not read. This layer-specific brittleness consistent with prior reports on late-layer readouts and LayerNorm-mediated self-repair in transformers~\citep{belrose2023eliciting,heimersheim2024how}. 

These pitfalls are not unique to chemistry foundation models. The NLP probing literature has flagged the underlying issue: probe accuracy is a noisy proxy for what a model actually uses~\citep{belinkov2022probing,pimentel2020information}, and probes decoding a feature need not identify the directions the model actually relies on~\citep{lasri2022probing}. Our results suggest that several features specific to scientific foundation models amplify these gaps in ways NLP probing rarely encounters, raising the bar for what counts as informative interpretability evidence in this domain.

\para{Three features of scientific FMs amplify the probing gap.}
First, \emph{tokenizer leakage} is substantially stronger. SMILES tokens often directly encode the property being probed: atom type corresponds to token identity, aromaticity is encoded by letter case, and chirality is partially marked by \texttt{@}/\texttt{@@}, so perfect embedding-layer probe accuracy on these properties reflects tokenizer design rather than learned compositional representations. Second, \emph{class imbalance} is severe: the QM9 majority class for formal charge is $0.97$ and for chiral tag is $0.795$, making naive accuracy uninformative without dataset-aware comparators. Third, \emph{multi-class intervention metrics} generalize awkwardly from binary NLP tasks: our cycle-target steering direction $\mathbf{W}_{c'}-\mathbf{W}_{c}$ does not always correspond to the basis the encoder itself uses, producing low intervention success even where probe accuracy is at $1.0$ (e.g., atom type).

Together, these features make standard NLP probing diagnostics unreliable as evidence of learned chemical structure in chemistry foundation models, and they motivate domain-tailored alternatives: dataset-aware baselines, tokenizer-aware controls, and causal-use metrics designed for the multi-class, ground-truth-rich, and structurally constrained nature of scientific representation problems. More broadly, different scientific domains may require different interpretability toolkits, depending on what structure is explicit in the input, what must be inferred, and how the model is expected to use that information internally.

Some properties appear at layer~0 because they are already specified by token identity, and these cases should not be treated as evidence of learned chemical abstraction. More informative benchmarks are contextual properties that require integration beyond the local token. Second, pretraining should be evaluated not only by final task accuracy, but also by whether it produces simple and persistent internal features that remain usable across depth. This perspective favors objectives and architectures that encourage early integration of relational structure rather than deferring chemically meaningful computation to the final layers. Third, the weak attention-distance correlations highlight a limitation of standard SMILES-only pretraining: strong predictive performance does not necessarily imply a spatially grounded internal representation. If spatial reasoning is the target capability, stronger geometric inductive bias or multimodal 3D supervision may be required.

\para{Future Directions.}
One natural next step is to study the internal geometry of chemical representations more directly. While linear probing identifies which properties are recoverable, it does not characterize how different chemical features are arranged relative to one another within representation space, whether they occupy shared or independent subspaces, or how these structures evolve across layers. Future work could examine feature overlap, representation superposition, and layerwise organization using methods such as sparse autoencoders, subspace analysis, or representation similarity techniques.

A second direction is to trace how chemical information flows through the network during computation. Our causal interventions show when a representation becomes functionally usable, but do not yet identify which downstream components consume that information or how multiple chemical features are composed into higher-level reasoning. Studying attention circuits, residual-stream interactions, and feature propagation across layers may help reveal how models combine local chemical descriptors into more global molecular representations.

Finally, an important open question is how different pretraining choices shape the resulting internal representations. Although \molm{} and \smi{} achieve similar downstream performance, they differ substantially in when and how chemical information becomes usable. Extending this analysis to additional architectures, objectives, and training datasets may help identify which design choices lead to more structured, transferable, or computationally efficient chemical representations.
