\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:res-disc}

\para{Chemical information can be present without being organized in a human-like way.}
The central lesson of this paper is not simply that chemical information is present in molecular foundation models, but that it is organized unevenly across mechanisms and depth. Attention shows only weak and selective alignment with geometric structure, while hidden states contain substantially richer contextual chemical information. At the same time, a feature can be linearly decodable before it becomes functionally usable by later layers. One broader implication is that successful chemical prediction does not require a model to organize chemistry in the same way a human chemist would describe it. These models may instead rely on distributed internal features that support prediction without corresponding to explicit, human-interpretable geometry-tracking mechanisms.

\para{Probing chemistry foundation models requires care.}
Our results also reinforce a key lesson from the probing literature: probe accuracy measures \emph{availability}, not \emph{use}. In \molm, several contextual properties become partially decodable by mid-depth, yet intervention succeeds only much later, near the top of the encoder. A linear probe alone would therefore suggest that the feature is already present in a useful form, while the intervention results indicate that it is not yet part of a computational channel used by later layers. The converse problem appears on chiral tag: the probe stays near the majority-class baseline, yet deep-layer intervention still flips the readout, which without a clean linear feature to perturb is more a property of the steering metric than evidence that the encoder has learned a usable chirality representation.

\citet{park2024linear} formalize this distinction: linear representations in a model's \emph{output} space, which probes read out, need not coincide with the \emph{input} directions that later computation actually uses. Related concerns appear throughout the probing literature. \citet{belinkov2022probing} survey the availability-versus-use distinction; \citet{pimentel2020information} cast probe accuracy as a noisy proxy for mutual information; \citet{hewitt2019designing} introduce control tasks to detect probes that succeed for the wrong reasons; \citet{antverg2022pitfalls} document the encoded-versus-used bug at the neuron level; \citet{elazar2021amnesic} test causal use by removing probe directions rather than perturbing them; \citet{geiger2024finding} formalize causal abstraction over neural representations and motivate distributed probe directions over axis-aligned ones; and \citet{lasri2022probing} show in NLP that probes decoding grammatical number do not always recover the directions BERT uses for agreement. Our results show the analogous gaps on contextual chemical properties in chemistry foundation models. The methodological lesson is that probes and interventions are most informative when used together and interpreted layerwise, rather than collapsed into a single summary number.

\para{Earlier functional accessibility may matter for downstream use.}
This distinction may matter most for downstream tasks requiring multi-step integration of chemical information. Properties relevant to ADMET prediction, reactivity, and molecular binding often depend on combining multiple local and non-local chemical cues rather than detecting a single motif. When a representation becomes usable early, subsequent layers can build on it as an intermediate computational feature for forming more global or task-specific representations. By contrast, representations that become usable only near the top of the encoder may still support final prediction, but have less opportunity to influence preceding computation. This may be especially important in practical transfer settings, where molecular encoders are commonly used through fixed embeddings, shallow predictors, parameter-efficient adaptation, partial freezing, or intermediate-layer readouts rather than fully end-to-end optimization~\cite{honda2019smiles,rollins2024molprop,cai2026chemfm,harod2025effichem,pinto2025unlocking}. Under these constraints, downstream performance may depend less on what information is eventually present in the model and more on which chemical features are already accessible, and at what depth.

\para{Scientific foundation models may require domain-specific interpretability methods.}
Our findings also suggest a broader methodological point: probing assumptions inherited from NLP carry over imperfectly to chemistry foundation models. First, \emph{tokenizer leakage} is more aggressive. SMILES tokens often \emph{are} the property being probed --- atom type is one token per element, aromaticity is letter case, and chirality is partially marked by \texttt{@}/\texttt{@@}. The perfect embedding-layer accuracy on atom type and aromaticity is therefore not evidence that the encoder learned those properties compositionally, only that the tokenizer made them explicit. Second, \emph{ground-truth class imbalance} is severe: the formal-charge majority class in QM9 is $0.97$ and the chiral-tag majority class is $0.795$, so accuracy against a uniform baseline is not very informative. Third, \emph{causal-intervention metrics} designed for binary NLP tasks generalize awkwardly to multi-class chemical properties: our cycle target $(c{+}1)\bmod C$ along the probe direction $W_{c'}-W_c$ does not always correspond to a channel the encoder uses internally, with the result that several multi-class properties report low intervention success even where the probe is nearly perfect.

Together, these issues suggest that scientific foundation models need more than direct transfer of NLP probing pipelines. They need probe protocols that explicitly account for tokenizer-encoded features, baselines tied to dataset structure rather than uniform majority class, and causal-use metrics adapted to the multi-class, ground-truth-rich, and structurally constrained nature of scientific representation problems. More broadly, different scientific domains may require different interpretability toolkits depending on what structure is explicit in the input, what structure must be inferred, and how the model is expected to use that information internally. Recent interpretability work on biological foundation models~\citep{lu2026mechanisms,goodfire2026evee,goodfire2026alzheimers} suggests that the field is already moving toward intervention-and-probe pairings tailored to each domain. We see this as a promising direction toward \emph{interpretability methodology specific to scientific foundation models}.

Some properties appear at layer~0 because they are already specified by token identity, and these cases should not be treated as evidence of learned chemical abstraction. More informative benchmarks are contextual properties that require integration beyond the local token. Second, pretraining should be evaluated not only by final task accuracy, but also by whether it produces simple and persistent internal features that remain usable across depth. This perspective favors objectives and architectures that encourage early integration of relational structure rather than deferring chemically meaningful computation to the final layers. Third, the weak attention-distance correlations highlight a limitation of standard SMILES-only pretraining: strong predictive performance does not necessarily imply a spatially grounded internal representation. If spatial reasoning is the target capability, stronger geometric inductive bias or multimodal 3D supervision may be required.

\para{Future Directions.}
One natural next step is to study the internal geometry of chemical representations more directly. While linear probing identifies which properties are recoverable, it does not characterize how different chemical features are arranged relative to one another within representation space, whether they occupy shared or independent subspaces, or how these structures evolve across layers. Future work could examine feature overlap, representation superposition, and layerwise organization using methods such as sparse autoencoders, subspace analysis, or representation similarity techniques.

A second direction is to trace how chemical information flows through the network during computation. Our causal interventions show when a representation becomes functionally usable, but do not yet identify which downstream components consume that information or how multiple chemical features are composed into higher-level reasoning. Studying attention circuits, residual-stream interactions, and feature propagation across layers may help reveal how models combine local chemical descriptors into more global molecular representations.

Finally, an important open question is how different pretraining choices shape the resulting internal representations. Although \molm{} and \smi{} achieve similar downstream performance, they differ substantially in when and how chemical information becomes usable. Extending this analysis to additional architectures, objectives, and training datasets may help identify which design choices lead to more structured, transferable, or computationally efficient chemical representations.
