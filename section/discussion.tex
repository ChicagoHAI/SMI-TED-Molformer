\section{Concluding Discussion}
\label{sec:res-disc}

Taken together, the attention and probing results suggest that these models do not encode chemistry in a uniform way across depth or mechanism. Attention shows only weak and selective alignment with geometric structure, indicating that spatial information is not cleanly represented through a small set of geometry-tracking heads. In contrast, probing reveals that several chemically meaningful properties are present in the residual stream, but that the depth at which they become functionally usable differs substantially across models. More broadly, our results take a step toward understanding not only whether chemical information is present, but how chemically relevant structure is organized across the model's internal representations and computations.

\para{The key difference between \molm and \smi is when chemical information becomes usable.}
The results suggest that \smi makes contextual chemical information functionally accessible earlier in the encoder, whereas \molm often does so only near the top. This distinction may matter most for downstream tasks that require multi-step integration of chemical information. Properties relevant to ADMET prediction, reactivity, or binding often depend on composing several local and non-local cues rather than detecting a single motif. If a property becomes usable early, later layers can build on it as an intermediate variable when forming more global or task-specific representations. If it becomes usable only near the top of the encoder, it may still support final prediction, but it has less opportunity to shape the preceding computation. This distinction may be especially relevant in practical transfer settings, where molecular encoders are often used through fixed embeddings and shallow predictors, partial freezing, parameter-efficient adaptation, or intermediate-layer readouts rather than unconstrained end-to-end optimization \cite{honda2019smiles,rollins2024molprop,cai2026chemfm,harod2025effichem,pinto2025unlocking}. Under such constraints, downstream training cannot freely reorganize the full encoder, so performance should depend more strongly on which chemical features are already accessible and at what depth. Layerwise accessibility may therefore be a useful predictor of downstream robustness, transferability, and data efficiency.

Some properties appear at layer~0 because they are already specified by token identity, and these cases should not be treated as evidence of learned chemical abstraction. More informative benchmarks are contextual properties that require integration beyond the local token. Second, pretraining should be evaluated not only by final task accuracy, but also by whether it produces simple and persistent internal features that remain usable across depth. This perspective favors objectives and architectures that encourage early integration of relational structure rather than deferring chemically meaningful computation to the final layers. Third, the weak attention-distance correlations highlight a limitation of standard SMILES-only pretraining: strong predictive performance does not necessarily imply a spatially grounded internal representation. If spatial reasoning is the target capability, stronger geometric inductive bias or multimodal 3D supervision may be required.

\para{Future Directions.}
One natural next step is to understand the geometry of these representations more directly. Our probes identify linearly accessible chemical properties, but they do not yet explain how those properties are arranged relative to one another in representation space, how stable they remain across layers, or how they interact with more global molecular features. A second direction is to study how these accessible properties are used in more complex prediction tasks that cannot be reduced to reading out a single local feature. For example, tasks involving activity, reactivity, or ADMET behavior may depend on combining ring structure, stereochemistry, valence, and longer-range context over multiple computational steps. Tracing how such intermediate chemical features are composed into higher-level predictions would help connect mechanistic analysis to practically important downstream behavior. More broadly, mechanistic analysis may provide a practical tool for chemistry model development by revealing what structure a model has learned, where that structure becomes available, and which pretraining choices make it more useful for downstream prediction.
