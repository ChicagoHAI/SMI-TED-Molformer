\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We study how molecular language models represent chemical structure at both the attention and hidden-state level. Revisiting prior claims that \molm attention captures spatial relationships between atoms, we find that attention correlates only weakly with true interatomic distance. Probing further reveals that \smi encodes contextual chemical properties more strongly than \molm, and that these representations are actively used during computation rather than merely correlated with the target properties.

\subsection{Attention-Distance Correlation}

    \caption{Mean attention-distance correlation per layer for \molm and \smi. Both models exhibit low correlations with \smi performing marginally better across layers.}
    \label{fig:attention_line_plot}
\end{figure}
\input{results_attention/stratified}

\para{Attention weakly reflects molecular geometry.} Although both models exhibit consistently positive mean correlations, all layer-wise mean Spearman correlations remain below $\rho \approx 0.3$ (Figure~\ref{fig:attention_line_plot}), suggesting that 3D geometry is only weakly encoded in attention patterns for both models. 

Overall, \smi exhibits consistently stronger correlations than \molm. Notably, the gradual increase in correlation across layers in \smi suggests that geometric information emerges progressively through the transformer hierarchy rather than being encoded uniformly at early layers. In contrast, \molm peaks at layer 6 before declining in later layers, suggesting that later \molm layers may instead capture more abstract or non-spatial molecular relationships.

\para{Attention exhibits local but non-uniform geometry alignment.}
Across both models, attention–distance correlations are strongest for short- and medium-range atomic interactions and weaken substantially at longer distances. \smi consistently exhibits consistently
stronger geometric alignment than \molm.
In the short-distance group, many \smi heads attain correlations between
$0.40$ and $0.50$, whereas the maximum short-range correlation in
\molm is $0.25$.
Similarly, medium-distance correlations in \smi frequently fall between
$0.35$ and $0.45$, while \molm medium-distance correlations peak at
$0.29$.
These results suggest that \smi preserves spatial structure more consistently than \molm.

Furthermore, several heads in both models exhibit negative correlations, suggesting that some heads capture chemical features not reflected by Euclidean distance alone, which may indicate that the models associate distance in the way human chemists do.
%This motivates the following linear probing experiments examining what if chemical properties are encoded in the hidden states.

\para{Comparison with prior \molm findings.} Our findings contrast with prior claims in \molm that attention strongly aligns with interatomic proximity. While both analyses compare attention weights with inverse pairwise distances, the original work aggregates attention across heads before computing cosine similarity with the inverse-distance matrix, potentially obscuring substantial head-level heterogeneity. In addition, cosine similarity measures global directional alignment, whereas our per-head Spearman analysis directly measures monotonic association between attention strength and spatial proximity. Our results instead suggest that geometric alignment is weak, highly non-uniform across heads, and largely absent for long-range interactions~\cite{ross2022largescalechemicallanguagerepresentations}.

\subsection{Linear probing of chemical properties}
\label{sec:res-probe}
\input{figure/probe_acc}

\para{Surface-form properties are recoverable at the embedding in both models.}
The probe accuracy for atom type (Figure~\ref{fig:acc-atom-type}) and aromaticity (Figure~\ref{fig:acc-is-aromatic}), two surface-form properties, is $1.0$ at the embedding layer ($\ell{=}0$) in both \molm and \smi and stays flat across all 13 layers, indicating these properties are directly encoded in the embedding and unchanged by any encoder computation.

%Formal charge (Figure~\ref{fig:acc-formal-charge}) sits operationally in the same regime: the QM9 majority class is already $0.97$ and probe accuracy stays in the $0.97$--$1.00$ band from the embedding onward in both models. Unlike the other two, however, this saturation is driven by dataset imbalance rather than tokenizer access.

\para{\smi encodes contextual properties more cleanly than \molm.}
The clearest cross-model gap appears on the contextual properties: ring membership, degree, chiral tag, hybridization, and total valence (Figure~\ref{fig:acc-is-in-ring}, \ref{fig:acc-degree}, \ref{fig:acc-chiral-tag}, \ref{fig:acc-hybridization}, \ref{fig:acc-total-valence}). The same \texttt{C} token can belong to a ring or not, can have different degree, and can carry different stereochemistry depending on its surroundings, so a probe that achieves high accuracy on these tasks must read information that the encoder put there from context.
\smi reaches near-perfect accuracy ($\geq 0.95$) on all five contextual properties. Total valence and chiral tag are already above this threshold at the embedding. Other properties also converge in the early layers. \molm's curves climb less steeply and plateau several points below. Ring membership levels off around $0.82$, hybridization around $0.84$, total valence near $0.97$, and degree at $\sim 0.58$ (only $0.15$ above the $0.43$ majority baseline). \molm's probe accuracy stays at $\sim 0.79$ across all 13 layers for chiral tag, essentially the $0.795$ majority-class baseline. This suggests the possible differences between how those two models encode contextual chemical properties.

\subsection{Causal intervention on chemical properties}
\label{sec:res-steer}
\input{figure/layer_steer}

Aromaticity (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-is-aromatic}) and formal charge (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-formal-charge}) show intervention success $\approx 1.0$ at every depth in both models, indicating that these properties are usable from the embedding onward and remain available throughout the encoder. This matches the probing results: when a property is already explicit in the representation, both models can route through it at essentially every layer.

The contextual properties show a different pattern. In \smi, intervention success rises rapidly and typically saturates by mid-depth, around layers~$6$--$7$. In \molm, the same properties improve more gradually and often do not reach ceiling until the top few layers, around layers~$9$--$12$. The two models therefore appear to use the same chemical concepts differently across depth. In \smi, contextual information becomes a usable computational feature relatively early, whereas in \molm it is incorporated more gradually and often only becomes fully usable near the top of the encoder. This suggests that \smi organizes contextual chemistry into actionable directions earlier, which may be advantageous for more complex downstream tasks that require later layers to build on ring structure, valence, or stereochemistry as intermediate features rather than only read them out at the end.

In \smi, the success curves additionally show a sharp dip at layer~$11$ across multiple properties. Sweeping $\alpha$ across both a fine grid in $[0, 1]$ and a wide grid up to $\alpha = 300$ at layer~$11$ never recovers the L10 / L12 ceiling for ring membership, degree, chiral tag, hybridization, or formal charge (Table~\ref{app:l11-table}). We therefore treat this effect cautiously: it is consistent with prior concerns that late-layer readouts and interventions can become brittle or misaligned with the model's own computation~\citep{park2024linear,belrose2023eliciting,heimersheim2024how}, rather than taking it as a robust model-specific scientific effect.
