\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We organize the analysis around two complementary measurements that probe different aspects of the same representation. A linear probe asks whether a chemical property is \emph{encoded} as a readable signal in the residual stream---it locates information. A causal intervention along the probe's decision direction asks whether that direction is \emph{used} by the rest of the encoder---it tests function. We present probing in Section~\ref{sec:res-probe} and intervention in Section~\ref{sec:res-steer}, then synthesize them through a four-cell framework in Section~\ref{sec:res-disc}.

\subsection{Linear probing: what is encoded}
\label{sec:res-probe}
\input{figure/probe_acc}

\para{Token-determined properties are recoverable at the embedding in both models.}
Atom type and aromaticity reach $100\%$ test accuracy at layer~$0$ in both \molm and \smi and remain flat across depth (Figure~\ref{fig:acc}). The SMILES tokenizer assigns distinct token ids to different elements and encodes aromaticity through letter case, so these properties are readable from the embedding lookup alone in both models. Formal charge sits in the same regime: with majority-class baseline already at $0.97$, both probes are at $0.97$--$1.00$ across all layers. Probe accuracy on these tasks reflects tokenizer design, not learned representation.

\para{\smi encodes contextual properties more cleanly than \molm.}
The clearest cross-model gap appears on ring membership, hybridization, degree, chiral tag, and total valence---properties that cannot be determined from a single SMILES token in isolation. \smi reaches macro-F1 $\geq 0.99$ by mid-layers on ring membership, hybridization, and degree, while \molm plateaus around $0.85$, $0.62$, and $0.87$, respectively. Identical \texttt{C} tokens belonging to ring versus non-ring atoms must be disambiguated by context, ruling out a pure tokenizer effect. The MLP probe (Appendix~\ref{app:mlp}) and the random-features control (Appendix~\ref{app:random}) confirm the gap is not an artifact of probe capacity: the MLP probe improves on the linear probe by at most $0.03$ accuracy across all (task, layer) pairs, and random-features probes collapse to majority-class performance. Given the architectures are nearly identical, the difference plausibly stems from pretraining producing more linearly organized representations of context-dependent structure in \smi.

The probe-only conclusion is, however, partial: it locates information but does not show that the encoder uses it. We resolve this in the next subsection.

\subsection{Causal intervention: what is used}
\label{sec:res-steer}
\input{table/layer6_intervene}
\input{figure/layer_steer}

\para{Setup.}
For each (task, source class $c$, target class $c'$, steer layer $\ell$), we form the steering vector $\mathbf{v}^{c \to c'} = (\mathbf{W}_{c'} - \mathbf{W}_{c}) / \boldsymbol{\sigma}$ in the original (unstandardized) hidden-state space using the layer-$\ell$ probe weights, where $\mathbf{W}_{c}$ is the probe row for class $c$ and $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ is the per-feature training standard deviation. We add $\alpha \cdot \mathbf{v}^{c \to c'}$ at every atom-token position whose baseline prediction is $c$, run the rest of the encoder, and read the layer-$12$ probe. \emph{Intervention success rate} is the fraction of atom-token positions whose post-intervention layer-$12$ prediction equals the intended target $c'$. For binary tasks the target is the only other class; for multi-class tasks we use a cycle target $c' = (c + 1) \bmod C$. Implementation details and the moved-off-source diagnostic are in Appendix~\ref{app:steering}. Single-layer interventions at $\ell{=}6$ already reproduce the probing-level ranking on contextual binary tasks (ring membership: $76\%$ on \molm, $100\%$ on \smi; Table~\ref{tab:steering-success}), confirming that \smi's contextual probe peaks correspond to channels the encoder maintains through the next six blocks.

\para{Sweeping the steer layer localizes \emph{where} information is causally used.}
A single-layer intervention can only confirm or deny encoder use at that depth; it cannot say where else the property is causally available. We therefore sweep $\ell \in \{1, \ldots, 12\}$ and report, for each (task, model, $\ell$), the intervention success rate at the best-performing $\alpha$ on the held-out molecules (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-layer}). The qualitative pattern across the contextual tasks is consistent.

For \smi on ring membership, the success rate is at $1.00$ from very early layers and stays there: the encoder treats the probe direction as a usable channel at \emph{every} depth at which the probe is accurate. \molm on the same task climbs steadily from $\sim 0.5$ at layer~$1$ to $1.00$ by layer~$9$, even though its probe accuracy is roughly constant ($\sim 0.83$) across that range. The probe finds a partial linear correlate at every depth, but only the deeper layers give the encoder a direction it routes through---the information is causally \emph{used} only at the top of the encoder. The same shape---near-flat \smi curves at high success versus \molm curves that lift only at the deepest layers---repeats on chiral tag, degree, and formal charge, in each case alongside a corresponding probing gap that intervention sharpens rather than overturns.

The token-determined tasks behave differently. Aromaticity flips at $\geq 0.99$ at every depth in both models, consistent with the embedding-level encoding. Atom type, hybridization, and total valence---all multi-class targets---show layer curves that are dominated by probe-prototype geometry rather than encoder use, and we discuss them in Section~\ref{sec:res-disc} as the diagnostic case for Hypothesis~B.

\subsection{Discussion: encoded versus used}
\label{sec:res-disc}

The two measurements jointly define a $2{\times}2$ framework. We use it both to interpret each task in our experiments and to articulate where probing and intervention can disagree.

\para{The four cells.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{High probe / high success} (encoded \emph{and} used). The property is linearly accessible and pushing along the probe direction changes the layer-12 prediction. Aromaticity in both models, and ring membership / chiral tag / degree / formal charge in \smi from mid-layers onward, fall here.
\item \textbf{Low probe / low success} (not encoded). The property is not linearly present and intervention cannot recover it. We do not see this case in our eight tasks; every task is at least partially encoded in both models.
\item \textbf{Low probe / high success} (used despite poor decoding). Not observed in our data; it would correspond to a property the encoder uses internally that no linear projection cleanly recovers.
\item \textbf{High probe / low success} (encoded but not used \emph{here}). The diagnostically rich case, with two competing explanations, A and B.
\end{itemize}

\para{Hypothesis A: the information is used at a different depth.} The probe at the intervention layer is reading a passive correlate; the encoder's actual computational channel for that property lives at another depth (typically deeper). The diagnostic is the layer sweep: a depth at which intervention success becomes high is a depth at which the encoder routes through the probed direction. \molm on ring membership instantiates this case: a roughly constant $\approx 0.83$ probe accuracy across mid-layers but intervention success that climbs from $\sim 0.5$ at layer~$1$ to $1.00$ by layer~$9$ (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-layer}, ring membership). The information is partially encoded throughout but causally used only at the top of the encoder. Chiral tag, degree, and formal charge on \molm follow the same shape.

\para{Hypothesis B: the probe direction is a probe-fitting artifact.} For multi-class probes, the steering vector $\mathbf{W}_{c'} - \mathbf{W}_{c}$ is a valid one-versus-other decision-boundary normal but has no guarantee of pointing at class $c'$'s centroid in the presence of other classes. Atom type illustrates this cleanly. Both models reach $100\%$ probe accuracy at every layer, yet \smi's cycle-target intervention success stays flat at $\approx 0.28$ from layer~$1$ to layer~$12$, while \molm's climbs to $1.00$. Per-source breakdowns at the layer-$12$ tautology resolve the apparent contradiction: \smi's intervention moves $100\%$ of carbons off carbon---the perturbation has full effect---but $0\%$ of them land on the cycle target fluorine; they land on nitrogen or oxygen instead. \smi's four-prototype layout at layer~$12$ is not arranged so that $\mathbf{W}_F - \mathbf{W}_C$ points at fluorine; \molm's layout happens to align with the cycle. The cross-model gap on this task is therefore a multi-class probe-prototype geometry artifact, not evidence about encoder use. Hybridization (3 classes) and total valence (4 classes) fall in the same regime in at least one model. We therefore restrict per-class causal claims to binary tasks and use \emph{moved-off-source rate} as the encoder-side propagation metric on multi-class tasks.

\para{Synthesis: \smi's pretraining gap is both representational and functional.}
Combining probing and intervention sharpens the qualitative claim made by prior work~\cite{soares2024smi, sharma2025foundation} that \smi's stronger downstream performance reflects better representations. Probing shows \smi encodes contextual properties as cleaner linear directions than \molm. Intervention shows those same directions are routed through every subsequent encoder block, whereas \molm's are routed only at the deepest layers (Hypothesis A). The advantage is therefore not located at any single depth or in either measurement alone: it is the joint property of being both \emph{cleanly organized} and \emph{causally used} throughout the encoder, which is what makes the representations more directly available to downstream heads.
