\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We study how molecular language models represent chemical structure and properties at both the attention and representation level. Prior work on \molm suggested that attention captures spatial relationships between atoms, even though SMILES strings do not explicitly encode 3D geometry. Revisiting this claim, we find that attention patterns correlate only weakly with true interatomic distances.
We then probe the residual stream for chemical properties and observe that while \smi encodes contextual chemical properties more cleanly than \molm, and those representations are not just correlated with the target properties, but are used during model computation.

\para{Overall.} Although both models exhibit consistently positive mean correlations, all layer-wise mean Spearman correlations remain below $\rho \approx 0.3$ (Figure~\ref{fig:attention_line_plot}), suggesting that 3D geometry is only weakly encoded in attention patterns for both models. Overall, \smi exhibits consistently stronger correlations than \molm. Notably, the gradual increase in correlation across layers in \smi suggests that geometric information emerges progressively through the transformer hierarchy rather than being encoded uniformly at early layers. In contrast, \molm peaks around layer 6 before declining in later layers

\para{Stratified.} The strongest correlations are concentrated in short- and medium-range ($\leq4$\,\AA) atom pairs, while correlations for long-range interactions ($>4$\,\AA) remain close to zero in both models (Figure~\ref{fig:attention_stratified}). This indicates that geometry-sensitive heads are driven primarily by local interactions, with comparatively weak encoding of long-range molecular structure.

\subsection{Encoding vs. Usage of Chemical Properties}

\input{figure/probe_acc}

\para{Surface-form properties are recoverable at the embedding in both models.}
Aromaticity (Figure~\ref{fig:acc-is-aromatic}) is surface-form properties, where can be read from the case of the letter (lowercase for aromatic atoms). The probe results reach $100\%$ test accuracy at embedding ($\ell$ = 0) in both \molm and \smi, indicating the properties are directly encoded in the embedding. Probe accuracy on surface-form properties reflects tokenizer design rather than learned representation, which aligns with how human reads the SMILE string. 

\para{\smi encodes contextual properties more cleanly than \molm.}
The clearest cross-model gap appears on the contextual properties: ring membership, degree, and chiral tag (Figure~\ref{fig:acc-is-in-ring}, \ref{fig:acc-degree}, \ref{fig:acc-chiral-tag}). The same \texttt{C} token can belong to a ring or not, can have different degree, and can carry different stereochemistry depending on its surroundings, so a probe that achieves high accuracy on these tasks must read information that the encoder put there from context. \smi reaches near-perfect accuracy by mid-layers on all three, while \molm plateaus around $0.62 - 0.85$.  Because chemical datasets are naturally imbalanced toward certain atoms and structures, \molm’s chiral-tag probe accuracy being close to the majority-class baseline suggests that the model is not effectively distinguishing different chiral states. \para{The two models use contextual properties at different depths of the encoder.}
The probe accuracy of aromaticity (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-is-aromatic}) is $1.0$ from the embedding layer, and intervention success remains $\approx 1.0$ at every depth in both models. The probe direction is therefore a usable channel throughout the network because the property is effectively encoded in the embedding itself.
Ring membership (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-is-in-ring}), degree (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-degree}), and chiral tag (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-chiral-tag}) show a different pattern. In \smi, intervention success rises rapidly in the early layers and reaches $1.0$ by mid-depth, matching the probe's near-perfect accuracy. This suggests that the encoder begins using the probed direction as a stable computational channel from the middle layers onward. In contrast, \molm's intervention success increases gradually and only reaches $1.0$ near the top of the encoder, even though probe accuracy stays roughly constant (around $0.85$) across the middle layers. The probe therefore identifies a partially linearly accessible feature throughout \molm, but only the deepest layers transform it into a direction consistently used by downstream computation.
