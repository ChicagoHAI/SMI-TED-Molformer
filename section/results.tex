\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We study how molecular language models represent chemical structure at both the attention and hidden-state level. Revisiting prior claims that \molm attention captures spatial relationships between atoms, we find that attention correlates only weakly with true interatomic distance. Probing further reveals that \smi encodes contextual chemical properties more strongly than \molm, and that these representations are actively used during computation rather than merely correlated with the target properties.

\para{Attention weakly reflects molecular geometry.} Although both models exhibit consistently positive mean correlations, all layer-wise mean Spearman correlations remain below $\rho \approx 0.3$ (Figure~\ref{fig:attention_line_plot}), suggesting that 3D geometry is only weakly encoded in attention patterns for both models. 

Overall, \smi exhibits consistently stronger correlations than \molm. Notably, the gradual increase in correlation across layers in \smi suggests that geometric information emerges progressively through the transformer hierarchy rather than being encoded uniformly at early layers. In contrast, \molm peaks at layer 6 before declining in later layers, suggesting that later \molm layers may instead capture more abstract or non-spatial molecular relationships.

\para{Attention exhibits local but non-uniform geometry alignment.}
Across both models, attention–distance correlations are strongest for short- and medium-range atomic interactions and weaken substantially at longer distances. \smi consistently exhibits consistently
stronger geometric alignment than \molm.
In the short-distance group, many \smi heads attain correlations between
$0.40$ and $0.50$, whereas the maximum short-range correlation in
\molm is $0.25$.
Similarly, medium-distance correlations in \smi frequently fall between
$0.35$ and $0.45$, while \molm medium-distance correlations peak at
$0.29$.
These results suggest that \smi preserves spatial structure more consistently than \molm.

Furthermore, several heads in both models exhibit negative correlations, suggesting that some heads capture chemical features not reflected by Euclidean distance alone. This motivates the following linear probing experiments examining what if chemical properties are encoded in the hidden states.

\para{Comparison with prior \molm findings.} Our findings contrast with prior claims in \molm that attention strongly aligns with interatomic proximity. While both analyses compare attention weights with inverse pairwise distances, the original work aggregates attention across heads before computing cosine similarity with the inverse-distance matrix, potentially obscuring substantial head-level heterogeneity. In addition, cosine similarity measures global directional alignment, whereas our per-head Spearman analysis directly measures monotonic association between attention strength and spatial proximity. Our results instead suggest that geometric alignment is weak, highly non-uniform across heads, and largely absent for long-range interactions~\cite{ross2022largescalechemicallanguagerepresentations}.

\para{Surface-form properties are recoverable at the embedding in both models.}
Atom type (Figure~\ref{fig:acc-atom-type}) and aromaticity (Figure~\ref{fig:acc-is-aromatic}) are surface-form: each element symbol is a distinct token, and aromaticity can be read from the case of the letter (lowercase for aromatic atoms). Probe accuracy is $1.0$ at the embedding layer ($\ell{=}0$) in both \molm and \smi and stays flat across all 13 layers, indicating these properties are directly encoded in the embedding and unchanged by any encoder computation. Formal charge (Figure~\ref{fig:acc-formal-charge}) sits operationally in the same regime: the QM9 majority class is already $0.97$ and probe accuracy stays in the $0.97$--$1.00$ band from the embedding onward in both models. Unlike the other two, however, this saturation is driven by dataset imbalance rather than tokenizer access.

\para{\smi encodes contextual properties more cleanly than \molm.}
The clearest cross-model gap appears on the contextual properties: ring membership, degree, chiral tag, hybridization, and total valence (Figure~\ref{fig:acc-is-in-ring}, \ref{fig:acc-degree}, \ref{fig:acc-chiral-tag}, \ref{fig:acc-hybridization}, \ref{fig:acc-total-valence}). The same \texttt{C} token can belong to a ring or not, can have different degree, and can carry different stereochemistry depending on its surroundings, so a probe that achieves high accuracy on these tasks must read information that the encoder put there from context.

\smi reaches near-perfect accuracy ($\geq 0.95$) on all five contextual properties: total valence and chiral tag are already above this threshold at the embedding, hybridization saturates by layer~$2$, ring membership by layer~$5$, and degree by layer~$6$. \molm's curves climb less steeply and plateau several points below: ring membership levels off around $0.82$, hybridization around $0.84$, total valence near $0.97$, and degree at $\sim 0.58$ (only $0.15$ above the $0.43$ majority baseline). Chiral tag is the starkest case --- \molm's probe accuracy stays at $\sim 0.79$ across all 13 layers, essentially the $0.795$ majority-class baseline. The cross-model gap is therefore not uniform: it ranges from a small total-valence margin to a near-complete absence of linearly recoverable chirality information in \molm.

\label{sec:res-steer}
\input{figure/layer_steer}

\para{The two models use contextual properties at different depths of the encoder.}
Aromaticity (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-is-aromatic}) and formal charge (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-formal-charge}) show intervention success $\approx 1.0$ at every depth in both models, mirroring their flat probe-accuracy curves: when the property is essentially recoverable at the embedding, the probe direction is a usable channel at every depth.

The contextual properties show a clearly cross-model pattern. In \smi, intervention success rises rapidly through the early encoder and saturates by mid-depth: ring membership (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-is-in-ring}) reaches $1.0$ by layer~$6$, chiral tag (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-chiral-tag}) by layer~$6$, and degree (Figure~\ref{fig:steer-degree}) by layer~$7$. In \molm, the same three properties climb gradually across the entire encoder and only cross $1.0$ at the top: ring membership at layer~$9$, chiral tag at layer~$11$, and degree only at layer~$12$. \molm therefore routes through the probed direction only at its deepest layers, even though its linear probe already reaches its plateau accuracy by the middle layers (Section~\ref{sec:res-probe}). The two measurements thus disagree about \emph{when} a contextual property becomes computationally available --- a dissociation between linear recoverability and causal use that we return to in the Concluding Discussion (Section~\ref{sec:res-disc}).
