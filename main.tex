\begin{abstract}
Large-scale molecular language models trained on SMILES strings, text-based representations of molecules, achieve strong performance on chemical prediction tasks, but it remains unclear how they internally represent molecular structure and properties. In this work, we analyze two widely used models, \molm~\cite{ross2022largescalechemicallanguagerepresentations} and \smi~\cite{soares2024smi}, from a mechanistic interpretability perspective. We examine whether attention patterns capture spatial relationships between atoms and use linear probes to track how chemical properties are represented across layers. We find that attention shows little correlation with true geometric structure. Probing reveals a key difference that \smi encodes contextual chemical properties in a nearly linearly separable way, while \molm only does so weakly, indicating less organized representations.
We then test causality by intervening on probe directions. These interventions reliably change predictions, showing that the probed features are causally used rather than merely correlated. Even \molm’s weaker signals are causal, but less accessible. Despite strong downstream performance, these results suggest that the models rely primarily on surface-level features rather than learning deeper chemical structure, highlighting a gap between predictive success and meaningful internal representations.

\end{abstract}
